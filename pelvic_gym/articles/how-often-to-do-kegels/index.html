How often should you do Kegels? Build a routine that fits

Understand the difference between a treatment dose, a reminder schedule and a sustainable long-term habit.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Many pelvic floor strengthening programmes use several short sessions each day, often three. That is a common framework, not a prescription for every woman. Follow the dose set for your symptoms and muscle function, include full relaxation between contractions, and seek advice if practice causes pain or difficulty emptying.

What to take away

  • Use a prescribed daily dose instead of collecting extra routines.
  • Spread planned practice through the day and include recovery between squeezes.
  • Missing a reminder calls for a better cue, not a marathon catch-up session.

Why do so many guides say three times a day?

For women receiving pelvic floor muscle training for stress or mixed urinary incontinence, NICE recommends programmes containing at least eight contractions three times daily, with a supervised trial of at least three months. This is guidance for treating specific conditions, not a universal starting challenge. See NICE's incontinence recommendations.

Other patient leaflets describe different numbers, positions and hold times. Reading all of them together can make it look as though you need to add the routines together. You do not. Choose one coherent programme with your clinician and ask how its details apply to your starting ability.

Write down all five parts of your dose

A useful plan answers more than “how often?” Keep these five items together so that a reminder tells you exactly what to do:

Part of the planQuestion to ask
PositionShould I practice lying, sitting or standing?
ContractionsHow many longer holds and quick contractions?
HoldHow long should each sustained contraction last?
RestHow long should I relax before repeating?
FrequencyHow many sessions each day, and when should we review?

Cambridge University Hospitals' exercise leaflet includes space for this individualized information and warns that the muscles can fatigue. It advises spreading practice through the day and resting between contractions.

If a timer runs faster than your prescribed rest, change the timer. If an online session includes more contractions than your plan, do not treat its completion badge as a medical target. The plan should determine the tool settings.

Build reminders around your real day

The Urology Care Foundation suggests linking practice to regular activities and setting aside attention while learning. Its pelvic floor exercise guide emphasizes developing a daily habit rather than finding a special time of day.

If your clinician has recommended three sessions, an example reminder schedule might be after breakfast, during a quiet afternoon break and before your evening wind-down. These are organizational examples, not extra exercise instructions. Choose moments when you can notice technique and finish the full rest periods.

  1. Pick the cue. Choose an event that happens reliably in your life.
  2. Make the instruction specific. Write “follow my pelvic floor plan” beside the cue.
  3. Keep the record simple. A tick plus a note about comfort is enough.
  4. Review the friction. If a cue repeatedly fails, change the cue rather than blaming yourself.

Privacy matters too. You can give a phone reminder a neutral label and keep symptom notes wherever you are comfortable storing them.

What if you forget a session?

A missed reminder gives you information about the schedule. Was the time inconvenient? Were the instructions hard to find? Did the exercise feel uncomfortable? Those problems need different responses. Write down the reason while it is fresh.

Return to your agreed routine rather than inventing a large catch-up dose. Ask your clinician how they want you to handle missed practice, especially if frequent interruptions make the prescription difficult to follow. Do not compress every planned session into one continuous block merely to maintain a streak.

If discomfort is the reason you are avoiding practice, pause and get advice. That is not a motivation problem. Queensland Health recommends professional help for pelvic-floor exercise pain, emptying difficulties or trouble making progress.

Rest is part of the exercise

The Victorian Government's Better Health Channel emphasizes being able to release the muscles fully between contractions. “More often” should not mean holding tension all day. Practice has a beginning and an end.

When the routine feels easier, make a note for your next review. Ask which part should change, if any: the position, hold, repetitions or maintenance schedule. Changing several things at once makes it harder to explain what you actually practiced. Our guide to how long Kegels take to work can help you track progress beyond a streak.

Common questions

Can I do Kegels every hour?

Do not add hourly sessions simply to accelerate results. Use the frequency prescribed for you, and get advice before increasing it. Continuous clenching is not the goal.

Can I practice on the toilet to remember?

Do not exercise by stopping and restarting urine. If a bathroom visit is your reminder, finish first and practice elsewhere. Leeds Teaching Hospitals advises against midstream exercise.

Will an app tell me whether I am doing them correctly?

A timer or reminder can organize a routine, but it cannot confirm an internal lift and release. If technique is uncertain, arrange an assessment.
