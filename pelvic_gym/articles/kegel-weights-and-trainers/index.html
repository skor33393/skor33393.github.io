Kegel weights and pelvic floor trainers: what to know before buying

Different devices solve different problems. Start with the purpose, evidence and suitability before comparing features.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Kegel weights, biofeedback trainers and electrical stimulators are different tools. None is automatically necessary or better than a well-taught exercise programme. A clinician can help identify whether a device suits your symptoms and technique. Pregnancy, recent surgery, pain or difficulty with insertion are reasons to get specific advice before use.

What to take away

  • A weight, sensor and electrical stimulator do different jobs.
  • A higher device score is not the same as fewer symptoms.
  • Check suitability, instructions and evidence before choosing a product.

First, identify what the device actually does

“Pelvic floor trainer” is a broad marketing label. A product might provide resistance, display a measurement, deliver stimulation or simply guide timing. Before comparing prices, find out which function you are buying.

ToolBasic purpose
Vaginal weight or coneProvides a load to retain during an exercise programme.
Biofeedback deviceShows information about a measured muscle-related signal.
Electrical stimulatorUses electrical stimulation to help produce a contraction.
Timer or reminderOrganizes practice without measuring the muscles.

Northampton General Hospital describes weights, feedback and stimulation as distinct treatment tools. A feature list should make these differences clear. If it does not, ask the manufacturer for the instructions before purchasing.

What does research say about weights?

A Cochrane review published in 2013 found some evidence that vaginal cones helped stress incontinence compared with no active treatment. It did not establish a clear advantage over pelvic floor exercise without cones. The trials were small and had limitations, and some women found cones difficult or unpleasant to use.

This is older evidence about a treatment approach, not proof that any current retail product will work for you. Ask whether a claim is based on the exact device, a similar device or pelvic floor training in general. Those are not interchangeable pieces of evidence.

Price, the number of weights and an attractive app do not answer the clinical question. Start with the problem you want to improve and the reason a weight might add value to your existing care.

More measurement does not guarantee better results

A 2025 Cochrane review found little or no difference in incontinence-related quality of life when biofeedback was added to pelvic floor training, and probably little or no difference in reported cure or improvement. That does not rule out an individual teaching role, but it does not support a universal promise of superior results.

Ask what a displayed score represents, whether it helps identify relaxation as well as contraction, and how a clinician would interpret it. Track the symptom or activity you care about alongside any device number. A rising graph is not a complete account of bladder control or comfort.

For a closer comparison of reminders and sensors, read Kegel apps versus biofeedback trainers.

Check suitability before inserting or switching on

Electrical stimulation has specific precautions. Leeds Teaching Hospitals' NMES guidance requires a physiotherapist to assess suitability and lists situations where its stimulator should not be used, including pregnancy, certain implanted devices and early recovery after birth or surgery. Do not apply a general shopping guide in place of those checks.

For any internal product, ask for advice first if you have pain, unexplained bleeding, infection symptoms, prolapse concerns or recent surgery. A device that is painful to insert is not something to “grow into” by forcing it. Check the exact product's restrictions and your clinician's instructions; one model's schedule should not be copied onto another.

Also distinguish a vaginal weight from a dilator used in some pain-treatment plans and a pessary fitted for prolapse support. Similar placement does not mean the same purpose.

A practical checklist before spending money

Make sure you can answer these questions in plain language:

  • What specific problem is this intended to help me address?
  • Who has checked that I can use it appropriately?
  • What does it measure or do, and what can it not tell me?
  • What are the instructions for cleaning, storage, use and stopping?
  • Can I review the instructions and return policy before purchase?
  • Does the connected app collect intimate health information, and can I control or delete it?

If a device is recommended, keep one agreed programme and confirm when it will be reviewed. Do not combine its routine with extra sets from other sources simply to accelerate progress. If the purpose remains unclear, a technique assessment is a more useful next step than choosing the most elaborate model.

Common questions

Do I need vaginal weights to strengthen my pelvic floor?

No. They are one possible training tool, not a requirement. Ask whether they add anything useful to your individual plan before buying.

Can I wear a Kegel weight all day?

Do not assume that longer use is better. Follow the specific product instructions and a clinician-approved programme; a training tool is not intended to replace normal relaxation throughout the day.
