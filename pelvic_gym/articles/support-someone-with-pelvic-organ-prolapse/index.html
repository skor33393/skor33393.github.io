How can I support someone with pelvic organ prolapse?

Offer useful help with daily tasks and appointments while keeping the person in charge of their care.

By Pelvic GymNovember 26, 20265 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Ask what kind of help the person wants, then offer something specific: a household task, transport, appointment notes or simply time to listen. Let them lead decisions about treatment and information sharing. Encourage a clinical review of new or changing symptoms instead of prescribing exercises or comparing their recovery with someone else’s.

What to take away

  • Offer a concrete task and an easy way to decline.
  • Help organize questions with permission; let the person speak for themselves.
  • Exercise apps are optional support for an agreed plan, not a substitute for care.

Start by asking what support would feel useful

A parent, partner or friend may want practical help, reassurance, privacy or a break from discussing symptoms. Ask before choosing for them. “Would it help if I took care of the shopping, came to the appointment, or just listened?” gives them a few real options without requiring a long explanation.

Accept a no. You can offer again at an agreed time without turning every conversation into a progress check. Avoid asking for intimate details to prove the problem is serious enough for support. The person can tell you what they need without describing an examination or sharing photographs.

Try reflecting the difficulty they describe: “That sounds frustrating. Which part of tomorrow would be easiest for me to take off your list?” This acknowledges the problem and makes the next action manageable.

Offer a small menu of specific help

Use this as a starting menu, then adapt it to the person’s preferences and any restrictions their own clinician has given. Do not turn it into a rule that they should stop all activity.

An offer such as “I can collect your groceries on Tuesday; would that help?” is easier to accept or decline than “Tell me if you need anything.” Keep the commitment realistic. Reliable small help is more useful than a large promise you cannot maintain.

Help them prepare for an appointment

The NHS advises seeing a GP for prolapse symptoms and explains that someone can ask for another person to be present during an examination. If you are invited to attend, clarify your role beforehand: transport, listening, note-taking or helping remember questions.

A short preparation note can contain three things in the person’s own words: the symptom or change, the everyday activity it affects, and the question they most want answered. Keep a separate space for the care team’s instructions and the next review date. Do not record the conversation without permission.

For example: “You said walking to the shops has become uncomfortable. Would you like that on your question list, or is there something more important today?” During the visit, let them answer first. If you think something was missed, ask whether they want you to mention it.

Our physiotherapy appointment guide explains consent and examination choices. Agree who keeps any notes afterward; medical information remains theirs to share.

Support the decision without becoming the prescriber

RCOG’s patient information describes several management options, including physiotherapy, a support pessary and surgery, depending on the person’s symptoms and circumstances. One person’s experience cannot tell your relative which option is appropriate.

You can help turn uncertainty into a question: “What are the benefits and drawbacks for my situation, and when should we review this choice?” Avoid treating surgery as inevitable, a pessary as failure, or exercise as something they simply need to try harder at.

If a home routine causes pain or there are difficulties with relaxation or bladder emptying, encourage them to contact their care team. Do not add contractions, a device or someone else’s exercise programme. If they use a pessary, the care and follow-up checklist can help organize questions for the fitting service.

When practical help includes getting medical advice

Offer help contacting the care team when symptoms are new, changing or interfering with daily life. You do not need to identify their cause before helping arrange a review. Avoid using a prolapse label to explain every new symptom.

NIDDK advises immediate medical attention for an inability to urinate or severe abdominal pain. Help the person seek urgent care in that situation rather than waiting for a routine appointment or trying an exercise session.

Would an exercise app be a helpful suggestion?

Only if the person wants one and strengthening is part of their agreed plan. Pelvic Gym offers guided pelvic floor workouts for iPhone. It may be a convenient practice aid when its routine fits the instructions they have received.

Ask before suggesting or setting up reminders. Do not use an app to monitor someone else’s compliance, and do not present a download as the solution to their diagnosis. The most helpful contribution may be giving them uninterrupted time for their chosen routine—or taking care of dinner while they rest.

The app cannot examine prolapse, fit a pessary or determine which treatment is suitable. Keep clinical follow-up separate from any optional exercise support.

Common questions

Should I remind my partner or parent to do Kegels?

Ask whether reminders are wanted and confirm that strengthening is part of their individual care plan. Repeated prompting is not helpful if the routine is unsuitable, uncomfortable or unwanted.

Should I stop them lifting anything?

Follow their own clinical advice and offer help with tasks they identify as difficult. Do not invent a blanket lifting ban or take over activities they want and are advised to continue.

Can I attend an appointment with them?

Ask the person first and check the service’s arrangements. Agree whether your role is transport, note-taking or support, and let them lead the conversation.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
