Can pelvic floor therapy help endometriosis pain?

Understand the role of pelvic health physiotherapy, its limits, and the questions that make a home plan more useful.

By Pelvic GymOctober 26, 20265 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Pelvic health physiotherapy may help address muscle tension and movement-related problems that accompany endometriosis pain. It does not remove endometriosis or replace assessment and treatment by your medical team. The appropriate plan may focus on relaxation and coordination rather than strengthening. Do not assume that pelvic pain means you need more Kegels.

What to take away

  • Ask what the therapist is treating and how it relates to your own symptoms.
  • A home exercise plan should follow assessment, not somebody else’s online routine.
  • A Kegel app can support suitable strengthening practice; it is not a treatment for endometriosis.

What can pelvic floor therapy actually address?

Pelvic floor therapy may help with muscle or movement problems that occur alongside endometriosis. Whether it is suitable depends on an individual assessment of your symptoms and needs.

Cambridge University Hospitals explains that muscles and joints can contribute to persistent endometriosis-associated pain. Pelvic floor muscles may become tense and painful, with effects on intercourse, bladder or bowel function. A pelvic health physiotherapist can assess that part of the picture and discuss suitable movement or relaxation work.

The distinction matters: supporting a painful muscle system is not the same as curing the underlying condition. The NHS endometriosis guide describes medical assessment and treatments such as medicines and surgery. Physiotherapy should fit alongside that care, with expectations agreed individually.

Why doesn’t the answer start with more Kegels?

Kegels train a pelvic floor contraction and release. NIDDK advises checking with a healthcare professional before starting, because they are not appropriate in every situation. Pain does not tell you that your muscles need stronger or longer contractions.

Before using a strengthening routine, ask: “Do I need to work on relaxing, coordinating, strengthening, or a combination?” If you have been told to avoid Kegels, an app reminder is not a reason to restart them. If an exercise increases pain or other symptoms, pause it and contact the professional guiding your care.

Our guide to tightness versus weakness explains why these are assessment questions. You do not need to work out the diagnosis yourself before asking for help.

What should I ask at the first appointment?

Pelvic health appointments usually start with a conversation about your concerns. An internal examination may be offered where relevant, but it requires your consent. University Hospitals Sussex explains the examination choices. You can discuss what it would add, ask about a chaperone, or decline.

Bring a few concrete examples: the activity that brings on pain, what you have already tried, and the task you would most like to make easier. Then ask:

  • Which of my symptoms might this treatment help, and which need my medical team?
  • What did the assessment tell you about how my muscles work?
  • What would a useful improvement look like for my own goal?
  • What should make me stop an exercise or contact you?
  • When will we review whether this approach is helping?

These questions are a preparation checklist, not a way to select your own treatment. See our first-appointment guide for more detail.

Can I do the exercises at home?

A useful home plan is one your therapist has chosen and explained for you. Ask to practice the instructions during the appointment, then repeat back what you understood. Record the movement, position, rest, frequency and any limits the therapist gives you.

For example, a helpful note might say: “I understand the starting position, but I cannot tell when I have released the contraction.” That gives your therapist something to check. It is more actionable than silently adding extra repetitions.

If cost or appointment availability is a barrier, tell the clinic. Ask about referral options, public or community services, the cost of an assessment, and how follow-up might work. Availability varies. Copying a routine from a stranger is not a substitute for knowing whether their problem resembles yours.

Choose a simple way to record how your agreed goal is changing. You might note whether sitting through a meeting is easier, alongside any symptom flare. Bring that record to the review; it does not need to be a perfect diary.

When should pelvic pain get medical attention?

Do not assume new or worsening pain is simply your usual endometriosis. The NHS pelvic pain guidance recommends emergency assessment for severe or worsening pelvic pain, particularly with faintness, difficulty breathing or heavy vaginal bleeding. Use your local emergency service.

Seek urgent medical advice for pelvic pain with fever, difficulty passing urine or stool, unusual vaginal bleeding or discharge, or if you are pregnant or may be pregnant. Persistent or recurring pelvic pain also deserves a medical appointment. An exercise session should not delay getting care.

Common questions

Does pelvic floor therapy cure endometriosis?

No. It may address associated muscle or movement problems, but it does not remove endometriosis or replace medical treatment.

How many sessions will I need?

There is no universal number. Ask what goals you are working toward, when progress will be reviewed, and what would prompt a change in the plan.

Should I do Kegels while waiting for therapy?

Do not start or intensify strengthening simply because you have pelvic pain. Ask a healthcare professional whether it is appropriate, especially if contracting makes symptoms worse.
