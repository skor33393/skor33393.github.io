Running and your pelvic floor: what to do about leaks or heaviness

A practical way to stay engaged with running while taking leakage, pressure and pain seriously.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Leaks, heaviness or pain during running deserve attention, but they do not automatically mean you must give up the sport. Describe when symptoms appear and seek a pelvic health assessment. A useful plan considers your running demands, muscle function and recovery, with changes tailored to your circumstances.

What to take away

  • Track the running conditions around symptoms, not only whether a leak happened.
  • Running readiness is individual, especially after childbirth or surgery.
  • A stronger squeeze is not the answer to every exercise-related symptom.

Which running symptoms should you mention?

Bladder leakage during exercise is one pattern of stress urinary incontinence. Pelvic pain, bowel leakage and a feeling of vaginal pressure are also symptoms to discuss. Wirral’s pelvic health service explains exercise-related leakage, and Continence Health Australia describes symptoms that affect activity.

Be specific: does it happen on the first few steps, near the end of a long run, during downhill sections or only in faster sessions? These observations do not diagnose the cause, but they make the conversation more useful than “running is bad for my pelvic floor.”

Think about response and coordination, not constant clenching

Healthy pelvic floor function includes contracting, releasing and responding to movement demands. Individual training may address strength, timing, relaxation or a combination. Continence Health Australia emphasizes this broader approach.

A small laboratory study found that pelvic floor activity changed around foot strike and increased with running speed. It does not establish a safe pace for an individual runner or prove that a particular stride prevents leaks. Read the original running study. Avoid turning every step into a deliberate maximal Kegel. If someone has prescribed a running-related muscle strategy, ask exactly when to use it and when to release.

Make a short running-and-symptom log

For the sessions you discuss with your clinician, use a few lines rather than an elaborate training spreadsheet:

  • Session: planned distance or duration, pace or effort, terrain and walk breaks.
  • Symptom: leakage, urgency, heaviness, pain or another change.
  • Timing: when it began and whether it continued afterward.
  • Context: recent changes in training, illness, recovery or your usual routine.
  • Impact: whether you stopped, changed the session or avoided running.

An example entry is: “Easy group run; pressure began near the end; I changed to walking; pressure remained when I got home.” Record what happened without trying to label the tissue involved. Bring the log and your main goal, such as returning to a weekly social run.

Discuss changes that preserve a useful activity routine

When symptoms affect exercise, assessment can help you make informed changes rather than abandoning activity without a plan. Continence Health Australia recommends tailored support.

Ask which change to try first: a shorter session, an easier effort, walk breaks or a different activity while you receive treatment. Treat these as discussion options, not a universal rehabilitation prescription. Change one feature at a time when possible so you can explain the result. Agree on what should make you stop and what would justify progression.

Useful questions include: “What can I comfortably keep doing?” “What are we trying to improve before increasing impact?” and “How will we review the response?” A plan should describe more than a ban or a return date.

If you run with a group, share only what you are comfortable discussing. You can request a shorter route or an easy way to leave the session without explaining intimate symptoms to everyone.

Returning after birth needs more than a calendar check

An international expert consensus recommends assessing postpartum runners’ pelvic health symptoms and considering broader physical readiness. It also notes that evidence does not establish a single pelvic floor strength score that guarantees running readiness. See the return-to-running consensus.

After childbirth, follow an individual recovery plan rather than copying another runner’s timetable. Pain, leakage or vaginal heaviness during or after exercise warrant stopping that activity and seeking guidance. Calderdale and Huddersfield NHS lists these warning symptoms. Our postpartum recovery guide helps you prepare that conversation.

Choose the next step based on the symptom

If leaks are your main concern, read our stress-leakage guide. For pain or difficulty relaxing, read why tight and weak symptoms can overlap. Do not use extra repetitions to push through an unexplained problem.

This article is educational, not a clearance to run through symptoms. The practical goal is a comfortable route back to the activity you value, with an assessment and review when needed.

Common questions

Does a leak mean running has damaged my pelvic floor?

A leak alone cannot tell you whether there is tissue damage or what is causing the symptom. It is a reason to assess the problem and discuss your running, not a diagnosis.

Should I hold a Kegel throughout my run?

Do not turn your whole run into a sustained maximal contraction. Ask a pelvic health professional about appropriate muscle training and any activity-specific cues for your situation.
