Kegel app or biofeedback trainer: what do you actually need?

Separate help following a routine from feedback about muscle activity before choosing a tool.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

A guided Kegel app helps you follow an exercise routine; a biofeedback trainer uses a sensor to provide information about muscle activity. They solve different problems. Pelvic Gym provides guided exercise plans on iPhone, not contraction measurement. If you are unsure of your technique or have symptoms, assessment is a better first step than guessing which device to buy.

What to take away

  • A routine on a screen does not measure your contraction.
  • A more expensive device is not automatically more effective.
  • Choose a tool around a clear need and an appropriate exercise plan.

Start with the problem you want to solve

“I forget my routine” and “I cannot tell whether I am contracting correctly” sound similar, but they call for different questions. The first concerns how you organize practice. The second concerns what your muscles are doing.

Write one sentence before browsing products: “I need help with...” Finish it without naming a brand. Add your budget and any practical requirements, such as avoiding an internal device or wanting instructions that are easy to read. This makes comparison more useful than choosing the longest feature list.

What a guided exercise app can do

Pelvic Gym offers guided exercise plans on iPhone. A guide can organize the timing and sequence of practice, but its on-screen progress is not a measurement of an internal contraction. Pelvic Gym should not be treated as a biofeedback device or a diagnostic test.

If you have a prescribed programme, compare its hold times, rest periods and repetitions with any routine you intend to follow. Ask your clinician about differences. A completed session means you followed the screen; it does not certify that the movement was correct or that symptoms should improve by a particular date.

What biofeedback adds

Biofeedback makes a body signal visible or audible. Kent Community Health explains a clinical system that reads pelvic muscle electrical activity through a probe and displays it on a screen. That system measures activity; it does not send a stimulating current into the muscle. Consumer products vary, so check exactly what a particular device measures.

Ask what its numbers mean, how placement affects the reading, and who will help interpret an unexpected result. A display is only useful if you understand the action being measured. Also check whether the product is a sensor, a weight, an electrical stimulator or a combination. These terms are not interchangeable.

Does adding biofeedback improve results?

A 2025 Cochrane review found that adding biofeedback to pelvic floor training made little or no difference to incontinence-related quality of life and probably little or no difference to reported cure or improvement. Most participants had stress incontinence. This does not rule out an individual use for feedback, but it does challenge the assumption that buying a sensor guarantees better results.

When a product cites a study, look for the exact device, participant group and comparison treatment. “Pelvic floor training helps” is not the same claim as “this particular product improves outcomes beyond training.” Testimonials cannot answer that comparison.

Five questions before paying

  • Purpose: am I buying guidance, measurement, resistance or stimulation?
  • Suitability: has someone explained whether this tool fits my symptoms?
  • Practical use: what setup, cleaning, storage and charging are involved?
  • Full cost: are subscriptions, replacement parts or consultations additional?
  • Privacy: what information is collected, and can I export or delete it?

For an internal device, discuss comfort, pregnancy, infection, allergies and any relevant procedures or devices you already have. Kent's clinical leaflet lists situations requiring caution or making its system unsuitable. Follow the instructions for the exact product rather than borrowing cleaning or safety directions from a different model.

If you are uncomfortable with insertion, say so. That preference belongs in the decision; it is not a hurdle you must overcome to take pelvic health seriously.

Where professional guidance fits

Imperial College Healthcare describes individualized treatment that may include education, exercise, biofeedback or other interventions when clinically indicated. Equipment is one possible part of care, rather than the starting point for every symptom.

Bring your proposed purchase to an appointment before ordering it. Ask what problem it would help address and how you would review its usefulness. For persistent uncertainty, our guide to pelvic floor physiotherapy explains the next step.

Common questions

Does Pelvic Gym detect whether I am doing a Kegel correctly?

No. Pelvic Gym provides guided routines; it does not measure pelvic floor contractions or replace a clinical technique assessment.

Do I need an internal trainer to begin?

No device is required simply to follow a guided routine. If you cannot identify the movement or have symptoms, discuss assessment and suitable treatment before choosing equipment.

Put your plan into practice

A little support for your routine.

When pelvic floor strengthening is right for you, Pelvic Gym’s guided workouts can help you make space for practice. Follow the plan and technique recommended by your healthcare professional.
