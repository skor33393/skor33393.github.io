Slow and fast Kegel exercises: what is the difference?

Understand the two common types of contraction without turning your routine into a speed challenge.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

A slow Kegel sustains a pelvic floor contraction before releasing; a fast Kegel contracts and releases promptly. Many strengthening programmes include both. Each repetition still needs a clear finish and adequate rest. The right hold length, repetition count and mix depend on your assessment, so use your own programme rather than chasing a universal target.

What to take away

  • A fast contraction still includes a full release.
  • Write down the rest interval as well as the hold time.
  • An unclear or painful movement needs review before more repetitions.

Two ways to practice the same movement

Slow and fast Kegels use the same basic inward lifting action with different timing. The Royal National Orthopaedic Hospital describes both sustained and quick exercises to train different muscle fibres. Its quick version includes letting the muscles open fully before contracting again.

Think of the labels as instructions about timing. “Fast” is not permission to skip the finish, and “slow” does not mean you should keep squeezing for as long as a timer can run. If you have been given only one type, ask why it was selected before adding the other yourself.

What a slow hold involves

A slow repetition has a start, a maintained contraction and a release. North Tees and Hartlepool advises breathing normally during the hold and relaxing afterward. Its patient leaflet also acknowledges that the suggested starting hold may be difficult for some people.

Use the hold length in your own plan. If you lose track of the contraction halfway through, record that instead of extending the next repetition to compensate. You might write: “I could feel the start, but was unsure whether I was still lifting near the end.” That is a useful technique question.

A timer measures elapsed time. It cannot tell whether the intended muscle action continued for that time. Keep that distinction in mind when a screen shows a completed hold.

What a quick contraction involves

For the quick version, North Tees describes lifting promptly and relaxing straight away. It also advises against holding the breath or using a large movement of the buttocks, legs or shoulders to complete the exercise.

Count a complete lift-and-release cycle rather than counting every tiny pulse. If the instruction feels confusing, say so at your next review. Ask your physiotherapist to demonstrate the rhythm verbally: where does one repetition end and the next begin?

There is no need to compete with a song, a video or someone else's speed. A useful session is one you can explain afterward, including the rest you took. If you cannot distinguish separate contractions, pause and clarify the technique.

Make the prescription easy to follow

University Hospitals Dorset's tailored exercise sheet records hold length and repetitions individually. It also includes resting between sustained contractions. You can use the same principle to make your own clinician's instructions legible.

  • Position: where should I practice?
  • Slow exercise: how long is each hold, and how many?
  • Rest: how long between contractions and between exercise types?
  • Quick exercise: how many complete contractions?
  • Schedule: how many sessions, and when will we review?

Leave unknown fields blank and ask for the missing details. Do not fill them by combining several online routines. If an app's preset differs from your prescription, your individual clinical instructions take priority.

When the mix needs a review

Tell your clinician whether difficulty occurs with the first contraction, only later in the set, during the hold or during release. Those are different observations. Bring the actual routine you followed, including any extra exercises from other sources.

The RNOH leaflet advises seeking assessment for pain, bulging or difficulty identifying the muscles. Do not respond to those symptoms by selecting a harder level. Our Kegel troubleshooting guide can help you prepare a specific question.

You also do not need to prove commitment through a perfect streak. If a routine is hard to fit into your day, discuss its practical demands. A clear, workable plan is easier to review than a changing collection of workouts.

Common questions

Are fast Kegels better than slow Kegels?

They have different timing and are often used together. Your clinician can explain the mix chosen for your needs; one type is not automatically an upgrade on the other.

Should I hold my breath during a longer squeeze?

No. Keep breathing normally. If that becomes difficult, pause and ask whether your technique or prescribed hold needs adjustment.
