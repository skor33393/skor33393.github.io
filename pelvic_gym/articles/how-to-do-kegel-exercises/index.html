How to do Kegel exercises: find, lift and fully relax

A step-by-step technique guide, including what the movement should feel like and what to do when you are unsure.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

To do a Kegel, gently close around the back passage and draw the pelvic floor inward and upward. Keep breathing, hold only as your individual plan allows, then fully release. Avoid clenching your buttocks, pushing down or practicing while peeing. If the movement hurts or remains unclear, get your technique checked.

What to take away

  • Look for an internal lift rather than a downward push.
  • Keep breathing and allow a complete release between contractions.
  • Use your prescribed hold and repetition count; technique comes first.

Before you start

This walkthrough is for learning the movement, not deciding whether strengthening is right for you. If you already have pain, difficulty passing urine, or a feeling that you cannot relax the area, speak with a pelvic health professional first. A tense pelvic floor can be difficult to contract effectively, according to Royal United Hospitals Bath.

Pick a quiet moment when you are not rushing. You do not have to complete a full workout to learn something useful. Your aim for this session is to distinguish three things: the start of the lift, the hold, and the release. Keep any instructions from your own clinician nearby.

If you cannot pass urine, seek immediate medical care rather than waiting for a physiotherapy appointment. NIDDK explains this warning sign.

Five steps to practice the movement

  1. Get comfortable. Sit with support or lie in a comfortable position. Let your legs and buttocks settle.
  2. Find the closure. Imagine keeping wind from escaping. Then bring your attention to the muscles around the vagina.
  3. Add a gentle inward lift. Think of the movement traveling into the pelvis, not pressing toward the chair.
  4. Keep breathing through a brief hold. Use the duration you were given. If you are still learning, do not force a long hold.
  5. Let the lift go fully. Notice the return to rest before considering another contraction.

This sequence follows the movement principles in NHS inform's pelvic floor guidance, which includes both sustained and short squeezes and emphasizes full relaxation.

Use one cue at a time. Repeating several mental images at once can make it harder to describe what you feel. If “lift” makes sense but “squeeze” does not, note that. Your clinician can use that information when checking your technique.

What should a Kegel feel like?

The sensation is generally an internal closing and lifting. Your body should not need a large visible movement to produce it. Cambridge University Hospitals advises avoiding buttock tightening, leg squeezing, abdominal bracing and breath-holding when learning the contraction.

A simple observation checklist can help you describe your attempt:

  • Did I notice an inward movement or a downward push?
  • Was I breathing comfortably?
  • Did my thighs or buttocks take over?
  • Could I notice a clear finish to the contraction?
  • Was there any discomfort?

This checklist is not a home diagnostic test. Feeling a movement does not prove that you are training every part correctly, and not feeling much does not establish weakness. If the answer remains uncertain, write “unclear” rather than marking the attempt as a failure.

How long should I hold, and how long should I rest?

Use your individual programme. Published routines differ because the appropriate starting point depends on what you can do. NICE says supervision should assess contraction and relaxation and tailor the programme to ability, discomfort, needs and goals. See NICE's supervision recommendations.

When you ask about dosage, include the rest interval. “Hold for five seconds” is incomplete if you do not know what follows. Ask whether your plan includes quick contractions as well as longer holds, and how to respond if the later repetitions become difficult. Do not turn peeing into a workout

Imagining that you are preventing urine flow can help explain which muscles are involved. Actually stopping and restarting urine is different. Do not practice this way: it can interfere with normal bladder emptying. Leeds Teaching Hospitals explicitly advises against using midstream stopping as an exercise.

Keep the cue and the practice separate. If bathroom trips are your reminder, wait until you have finished, then use a different place for your planned practice. There is no need to repeatedly test your strength on the toilet.

If you cannot tell whether it is working

Ask a pelvic health physiotherapist or another appropriately trained clinician to check. You can say: “I would like to know whether I am lifting and fully relaxing, and what dose fits me.” An assessment may include an examination with your consent; you can ask what is proposed and discuss alternatives. Kingston and Richmond NHS explains this consent-based approach.

Stop if practice causes pain. You do not need to earn a review by completing several weeks of uncomfortable exercise. If you are ready to troubleshoot a specific habit, read common Kegel exercise mistakes.

Common questions

Should I hold my breath during a Kegel?

No. Keep breathing during the lift and hold. If you cannot do that comfortably, pause and ask for help adjusting the exercise rather than forcing the count.

Do I need to insert a finger to learn?

No. You can begin with verbal movement cues and ask a professional for guidance. Any intimate assessment should be explained and carried out only with your consent.
