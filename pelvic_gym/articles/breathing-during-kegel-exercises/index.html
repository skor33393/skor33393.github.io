How to breathe during Kegel exercises without overthinking it

A clear way to understand breath cues, longer holds and the pause between contractions.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Keep breathing during Kegel exercises. When learning coordination, you can begin the lift on an exhale and release on an inhale. A longer hold may span several breaths, so do not hold your breath to match the squeeze. Follow your individual programme, and ask for help if contraction or release feels uncomfortable or unclear.

What to take away

  • The contraction and your breathing do not have to last the same length of time.
  • A longer hold should still allow breathing.
  • Release fully between repetitions; pain is a reason to pause and seek advice.

What does “exhale and lift” mean?

Royal United Hospitals Bath describes beginning a pelvic floor lift while breathing out, then releasing with the next inward breath. This is a coordination cue for learning, rather than a demand to empty your lungs completely.

Use the words in a way you can follow comfortably. If you are concentrating on four instructions at once—counting, lifting, breathing and watching a screen—simplify the task and clarify the confusing part. A useful first question is whether the difficulty is starting the lift or keeping your breathing going afterward.

Notice breathing before adding a contraction

Wirral Community Health suggests a comfortable supported position and placing a hand near the lower ribs or belly to notice movement with breathing. Its pelvic floor guidance pairs the outward breath with activation and the inward breath with relaxation.

You can begin by simply observing a few comfortable breaths without doing Kegels. There is nothing to score. Notice whether you are rushing to start the next instruction or trying to manufacture a very large breath. The goal is a pattern you can comfortably return to, not a dramatic visible movement.

If you have been given a particular position because of pregnancy, pain or surgery, keep using it. This breathing cue does not require lying flat or changing your existing restrictions.

What if the hold is longer than one breath?

A sustained contraction can continue while you breathe. The Bath leaflet describes progressing to a hold through a further breathing cycle before releasing. It leaves hold length and repetition count to the person's physiotherapist.

Suppose your written plan calls for a hold longer than one comfortable exhale. The instruction is not to stretch that exhale until you run out of air. Ask your clinician to help you coordinate ordinary breathing through the hold. If you find yourself taking a big recovery gasp afterward, record that observation.

Keep the two jobs separate in your notes: “hold duration” and “breathing stayed comfortable.” Reaching the number on the timer does not settle the second question.

Give the release its own attention

After the lift, allow the muscles to return to rest. You do not need to push downward to make a release happen. If you cannot tell whether you have stopped contracting, bring that uncertainty to a technique check.

St George's muscle-overactivity guidance explains that difficulty relaxing can coexist with weakness, and treatment may need to address tension first. More strengthening is therefore not the automatic answer to an unclear or uncomfortable sensation.

Ask for the rest interval to be written down alongside the hold. A plan that only says how long to squeeze can leave you guessing about half of the exercise. Do not let an app countdown persuade you to continue through pain.

Troubleshoot one issue at a time

  • You stop breathing while counting. Note when it happens and ask whether a different counting cue or shorter prescribed hold would help.
  • You lose the contraction when inhaling. Describe that exact moment at your review rather than compensating with a harder squeeze.
  • You keep gripping after the timer ends. Ask for help identifying the release before adding repetitions.
  • The exercise hurts. Pause and seek advice about whether strengthening is appropriate.

These observations are useful even if you cannot name the muscles or explain the anatomy. You can read your notes directly at an appointment. There is no need to turn breathing practice into a test you must pass alone.

Keep your routine simple

Choose one breathing cue that agrees with your clinician's instructions and keep it beside your programme. Avoid changing cues every day in response to a new video. When you review progress, bring the cue as well as the repetition count.

For example: “I start on the exhale, but the third hold makes me stop breathing.” That tells your clinician more than “I did all the exercises.” Our guides to slow and fast contractions and practice positions explain the other details you may want to clarify.

Common questions

Must every Kegel fit into one breath?

No. Longer holds can span several breaths. Use the timing in your individual programme while continuing to breathe comfortably.

Does breathing alone replace a strengthening programme?

Breathing practice and strengthening have different purposes. Follow the plan chosen for your symptoms and ask before substituting one for the other.
