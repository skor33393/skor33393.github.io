Pelvic floor exercises during menopause: what they can help with

Understand where pelvic floor training fits during menopause—and which symptoms need a different conversation with your clinician.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Pelvic floor exercises may help some bladder-control problems during menopause, but they do not address every symptom. Dryness, burning, painful sex, urgency and recurrent urinary infections need their own assessment. A useful plan considers muscle function, tissue changes and your priorities, rather than assuming all changes require more Kegels.

What to take away

  • Menopause-related symptoms can involve both muscles and vaginal or urinary tissues.
  • A training plan should include full relaxation and a review point.
  • Vaginal bleeding after menopause needs medical assessment, even if it happens once.

Why symptoms can change around menopause

As estrogen levels fall, vaginal and urinary tissues can change. Dryness, burning, discomfort during sex and urinary symptoms may be part of genitourinary syndrome of menopause, often shortened to GSM. ACOG explains these changes.

That gives you useful language for a medical appointment, but it should not become a catch-all explanation. A symptom that begins in midlife still deserves assessment. Instead of saying only “I think this is menopause,” describe exactly what has changed and what you want help with: sleeping without repeated bathroom trips, returning to exercise or making intimacy comfortable.

Separate the symptoms you want to address

Make three short headings in your notes: leakage, urgency or frequency, and discomfort or dryness. Add examples under each that applies. You can also record what you have tried and whether it helped. The point is to avoid squeezing several different concerns into one vague phrase such as “bladder weakness.”

Lower estrogen can contribute to urinary urgency and recurrent urinary tract infections, according to the U.S. Office on Women’s Health. A clinician can help decide what needs investigation and what treatment fits. Ask whether more than one issue might be contributing, particularly if a single approach has not helped.

Where pelvic floor training can fit

The pelvic floor helps support the pelvic organs and maintain bladder closure during activities such as coughing. Menopause and aging can contribute to reduced muscle function. Cambridge University Hospitals describes the role of pelvic floor exercises.

If strengthening is recommended, focus on a clear contraction, normal breathing and a complete release. Avoid clenching the buttocks, squeezing the legs together or holding your breath. A physiotherapist can help you check technique. See the CUH technique guidance.

Our step-by-step Kegel guide explains the movement, and the frequency guide explains why a manageable plan matters. If the exercise is painful or hard to release, seek advice before increasing it.

Dryness and irritation need their own treatment discussion

Moisturizers, lubricants and prescription treatments address different aspects of GSM. NICE recommends discussing vaginal estrogen for menopause-associated genitourinary symptoms and also describes non-hormonal options. Individual medical history matters; a history of breast cancer calls for a more specific discussion, sometimes with the cancer team. Read NICE’s recommendations.

Pelvic floor squeezing is not a treatment for thinning or dryness of vaginal tissue. Ask which symptom each proposed treatment is intended to improve, how you will use it and when to review. You do not have to choose between discussing exercise and discussing tissue comfort; both can be part of the appointment.

Build a plan you can actually review

Choose one meaningful outcome to discuss with your clinician. For example, “I want to walk with my friend without worrying about a leak.” Then agree on the steps and a review point. A reminder can support an exercise plan, but completing a streak is different from feeling better in daily life.

Keep your record brief: exercises completed, any discomfort, and the activity you care about. If your symptoms change, bring that information to the review. Do not treat a difficult week as a reason to double the exercise dose. Ask what to change, especially if your original plan no longer fits your symptoms.

If urgency or discomfort is your main concern, read why symptoms do not always mean a weak pelvic floor before assuming that more strengthening is the answer.

When to seek medical advice

Any vaginal bleeding after menopause should be checked, including a small amount or a single episode. It often has a non-cancerous cause, but assessment is needed. The NHS explains postmenopausal bleeding. Do not assume spotting is simply dryness or an exercise effect.

Arrange assessment for new or bothersome urinary and pelvic symptoms rather than waiting for a routine menopause appointment. This article provides education; it does not choose hormonal treatment or an exercise prescription for you.

Common questions

Am I too old to start pelvic floor exercises?

Age alone is not a reason to skip a discussion about pelvic floor care. Ask whether training fits your symptoms and how to begin at an appropriate level.

Will Kegels help vaginal dryness?

Kegels do not replace treatment for vaginal dryness or tissue changes. Discuss moisturizers, lubricants and other suitable options with your clinician, especially if you also have pain or urinary symptoms.
