Pelvic floor exercises after a C-section: when and how to restart

Caesarean recovery includes the pelvic floor as well as the abdominal incision. Start with the guidance for your own birth.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Pelvic floor care can still matter after a C-section because pregnancy also affects the pelvic floor. Gentle exercises may begin when comfortable and advised by your maternity team, after any urinary catheter is removed. Difficulty passing urine, pain or complications change the plan; a generic workout should not override your discharge instructions.

What to take away

  • A C-section does not make pelvic floor symptoms irrelevant.
  • Wait until a urinary catheter is removed and report emptying problems.
  • Restarting gentle practice is different from returning to strenuous exercise.

Why the pelvic floor still matters

A caesarean avoids a vaginal birth, but the pelvic floor has still supported a pregnancy. You may have questions about leaks, pressure or muscle control even if the abdominal incision is the most obvious part of recovery. Lancashire Teaching Hospitals' caesarean information includes pelvic floor rehabilitation for this reason.

There is no need to compare yourself with someone who had a different delivery, or to interpret symptoms as evidence that your birth “should have prevented” them. Start with what you notice and what your team has advised. Write down whether the issue is leaking, discomfort, uncertainty about movement or difficulty emptying, because each deserves a clear description.

When can I restart gentle exercises?

University College London Hospitals includes gentle pelvic floor exercise after caesarean birth and says to wait until a urinary catheter has been removed. Your own maternity team's instructions take priority, particularly if the birth or recovery was complicated.

Before you begin, check three practical points: whether your catheter is out, whether you are passing urine normally, and whether you have been given any individual restrictions. If emptying is difficult, tell the team rather than trying extra squeezes. Ask for help if you are unsure what they mean by “gentle.”

Getting permission for pelvic floor practice is not the same as being ready for a strenuous workout. Keep those decisions separate when asking about recovery.

Make the first practice about clear movement

In a comfortable supported position, the movement is a small internal lift followed by a complete letting-go. Keep breathing. Use the amount of practice your clinician recommends and stop if it hurts. UCLH's post-birth exercise advice also warns against exercising by stopping and starting urine flow.

You may find it useful to separate learning from counting. First understand what the intended movement feels like; then write down the routine you have been given. Do not try to demonstrate progress by making a forceful abdominal brace or finishing a timer despite discomfort.

If sensation is unclear, describe that at your next contact. “I cannot tell when the lift ends” is a useful question. Our Kegel technique guide explains the language, while an assessment checks what your body is doing.

Fit pelvic floor care into the whole recovery plan

The NHS caesarean recovery guide encourages gentle movement while cautioning against overexertion. Activities such as lifting more than your baby or returning to exercise may need to wait until you feel able and they are comfortable; your midwife can advise.

For planning, divide your day into essential care, optional activity and tasks someone else can do. Feeding your baby, getting to the toilet and resting may already require substantial organization. Put a brief prescribed practice into a manageable gap rather than making it compete with an ambitious fitness schedule.

  • Keep discharge instructions and contact details together.
  • Ask for help with shopping, laundry and older children's needs.
  • Record the activity connected to a symptom, not only its intensity.
  • Discuss changes before adding weights, internal devices or demanding exercise.

Choose one person who can help keep track of appointments or instructions if you are tired. They can write down questions while you focus on your recovery and your baby.

Which symptoms should I report?

Contact your maternity team urgently about painful urination, worsening wound redness, pus, severe pain, heavy bleeding, or new calf pain or swelling. Report urine leakage too. These are among the symptoms the NHS says need medical advice after caesarean birth. They should not be treated as a normal response to pelvic floor practice.

New chest pain or sudden breathlessness after surgery needs immediate medical help; call your local emergency service. NHS guidance on pulmonary embolism flags these symptoms as possible signs of a clot in the lungs.

For ongoing non-urgent concerns, use a review to agree the next step: technique support, pelvic health physiotherapy, or a change in the recovery plan. Read our wider postpartum recovery guide for questions to take with you.

Common questions

Do I need to wait six weeks for every pelvic floor exercise?

Not necessarily. Gentle pelvic floor practice and strenuous activity have different considerations. Follow your maternity team’s advice, wait for catheter removal, and raise pain or emptying problems before starting.

Will Kegels help my C-section scar heal?

Pelvic floor practice is not a substitute for incision care or a full postoperative plan. Follow the wound-care instructions and report changes that concern you.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
