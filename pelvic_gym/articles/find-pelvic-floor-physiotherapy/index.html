Getting started / A Pelvic Gym guide

How to find pelvic floor physiotherapy that fits your needs

Make the first step smaller: identify the barrier, ask the right service questions and plan a useful appointment.

By Pelvic GymOctober 29, 20265 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Start with a clinician or local pelvic health service and ask how referrals work for your symptoms. Before booking, check relevant experience, total costs, appointment format and what an assessment may involve. Tell the service about practical or comfort needs early; you do not have to agree to an examination just to ask for help.

What to take away

  • Access routes vary by service and location; check the actual eligibility rules.
  • Ask about costs, travel, privacy and support needs before committing to a plan.
  • Pain and difficulty emptying need assessment; an exercise app does not replace it.

Identify the barrier you need to solve

“I cannot get pelvic floor care” can mean several different problems: no nearby clinician, a long wait, cost, limited appointment times, uncertainty about an internal examination, or a previous visit that felt unhelpful. Name the biggest barrier first. The next step for travel is different from the next step for consent or cost.

Write a two-sentence summary: the symptom that affects you most and the obstacle to attending. For example, “Bladder symptoms interrupt my workday. I need to know whether you offer an initial appointment outside working hours.” You do not need a self-diagnosis to contact a service.

Check the local referral route

Start with your primary-care clinician, an existing specialist or the local pelvic health service. Ask whether you need a referral, which symptoms it accepts and whether there are age, location or pregnancy-related eligibility rules. Insurance arrangements also need checking with the service and insurer.

UK services illustrate why one answer does not fit everyone: Mid Cheshire requires a healthcare-professional referral, while Doncaster and Bassetlaw describes a self-referral route for eligible pregnancy-related problems. These are examples, not promises about access where you live.

If a service cannot see you, ask which service handles your main symptom. Request the name or contact route so that you are not left searching for a vaguely defined specialist.

Five questions to ask before booking

TopicQuestion to ask
ExperienceDo you regularly assess the symptoms I have?
CostWhat does the first appointment cost, and are tests or equipment charged separately?
Follow-upHow are further visits recommended and progress reviewed?
AccessWhat appointment formats and accessibility arrangements are available?
CoordinationHow will you communicate with my other clinician if needed?

Check the clinician’s professional registration through the relevant regulator where you live. A directory entry, social-media following or confident promise is not a substitute for checking qualifications and the scope of the service.

If money is the main constraint, say so before booking. Ask about public or insurance-funded routes, payment arrangements, and what a limited number of visits could reasonably cover. Do not assume that buying a package is required before an assessment.

Ask what a remote appointment can answer

Some services offer telephone, group and face-to-face options; Doncaster and Bassetlaw describes these formats. Ask what your proposed remote appointment includes and what would require an in-person examination. Availability does not establish that one format is suitable for every symptom.

Before a remote visit, check whether you have a private place to speak, the equipment you need and a way to receive a written plan. Ask how the clinician handles symptoms needing prompt medical assessment. Do not assume an online coaching package provides the same assessment as a regulated clinical service.

Explain what would make an appointment feel manageable

You can ask in advance about an initial conversation, a chaperone, an interpreter or additional support needs. Mid Cheshire explains that an internal examination is discussed and consent obtained, and that patients may decline. Ask your own clinician what alternatives would be possible and what information they could or could not provide.

A useful opening is: “I am nervous about an examination. Please explain each proposed step before I decide.” You can also ask for a pause. If a previous appointment was difficult, you can describe what you need now without retelling every detail.

Make a plan for the waiting period

Ask who to contact if symptoms change while you wait, and whether a cancellation appointment is an option. Keep a short symptom note and the details of previous treatment. Avoid assembling several exercise programmes to compensate for the wait, especially when you have pain or trouble emptying.

Seek immediate medical care if you cannot urinate or have severe abdominal pain, as advised by NIDDK. Severe or worsening pelvic pain, or pelvic pain with faintness, needs emergency assessment. The NHS lists urgent and emergency pelvic-pain signs. A routine physiotherapy waiting list should not delay that care.

Use an app only for the part of the plan it supports

Once you have a plan, ask what each exercise is meant to train, how to recognize a comfortable release and when to stop or seek review. Pelvic Gym offers guided strengthening workouts for people whose clinician recommends that approach. It cannot choose the cause of symptoms, examine your pelvic floor or replace individualized relaxation treatment.

If strengthening fits your assessment, bring the app routine to the clinician and confirm it matches the plan before making it a habit.

Common questions

Do I always need a referral?

No universal rule applies. Requirements depend on the service, location, symptoms and payment arrangements. Check with the clinic and insurer where relevant.

Can I ask for help if I am not ready for an internal examination?

Yes. Explain this when booking and ask what an initial conversation or alternative assessment can cover. The clinician should explain proposed examinations and obtain consent.

Can a pelvic floor app replace the first appointment?

An app cannot examine you or determine why you have pain, leakage or difficulty emptying. Use a strengthening app only when that exercise approach fits your care plan.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
