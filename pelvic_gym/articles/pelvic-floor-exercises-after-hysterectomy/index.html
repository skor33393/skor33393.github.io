Pelvic floor exercises after hysterectomy: restarting with your care plan

Use your operation details and discharge advice to restart gentle practice and plan a gradual return to daily life.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

After hysterectomy, gentle pelvic floor exercises may be introduced once a urinary catheter is removed and you can pass urine normally, following your surgical team’s advice. The operation type, additional repairs and complications matter. Early muscle practice does not mean internal devices, heavy lifting or strenuous exercise are appropriate.

What to take away

  • Your exact operation and discharge instructions determine the next step.
  • Do not practice with a urinary catheter in place or ignore emptying problems.
  • Gentle muscle practice and the return to heavy activity are separate decisions.

Start with the operation you actually had

A hysterectomy can be performed through an abdominal incision, vaginally or using laparoscopy. Recovery varies, and additional procedures can change your restrictions. The NHS recovery guide describes different hospital stays and recovery expectations by approach. A date from someone else's experience is not a reliable clearance for you.

Before leaving hospital, keep a copy of the operation and discharge information. Check whether you had a prolapse repair or another procedure alongside the hysterectomy. These details help anyone advising you later understand which tissues are healing and which instructions apply.

If a leaflet and a verbal instruction appear to conflict, ask the surgical team to resolve it. Do not choose the more demanding option simply because you feel keen to get moving.

When might gentle practice restart?

Leeds Teaching Hospitals advises starting pelvic floor exercises gently after the catheter is removed and you can pass urine independently. If you still have a vaginal pack, follow the team's instructions about its removal before practice. Doncaster and Bassetlaw's postoperative guidance specifically says not to start while a catheter or pack remains in place.

Ask the ward team to confirm your starting point rather than trying to remove or work around any device yourself. If urination is difficult after catheter removal, report it. Pelvic floor contractions are not a way to force the bladder to empty.

Your first programme may be modest. The purpose is to regain an appropriate movement, not to prove how much effort you can tolerate immediately after surgery.

What should the movement involve?

North Bristol NHS Trust describes an inward and upward contraction around the back passage and vagina, followed by a release to the starting position. It also advises against training by interrupting the urine stream.

Use the hold, rest and repetition count your clinician gives you. Ask for a demonstration or technique check if the words are unclear, and pause if an exercise causes pain. Keep the rest interval in your written instructions: it is easy to remember the number of squeezes and forget what happens between them.

Do not add vaginal weights, a probe or an internal stretching routine because it appears in a general recovery video. Obtain specific advice about when vaginal insertion is appropriate after your operation. External muscle practice and inserting a device are different activities.

Plan daily activity separately from exercise repetitions

The NHS encourages early short walks as part of postoperative care while emphasizing the time needed for tissue healing. “I can do my pelvic floor practice” does not answer whether you can lift a full laundry basket, resume your gym class or return to a physical job.

Make a practical list of the jobs waiting at home. Include pet care, shopping, changing bedding and anything involving children. Ask which tasks need help and for how long. Agree a way to increase activity and a review point, rather than treating one good morning as clearance for everything.

Ask about constipation management as well. RCOG's abdominal hysterectomy guide discusses keeping stools easy to pass and the possible need for laxatives. Follow the medication advice you were given; do not strain to finish a bowel movement.

What needs a call rather than another workout?

Contact your surgical service promptly for increasing abdominal pain, heavy or unpleasant-smelling bleeding, wound redness or painful urination. Fever or vomiting alongside worsening pain also needs assessment. RCOG lists these in its postoperative warning signs. New chest pain, breathlessness or coughing blood requires immediate medical help.

At a routine review, discuss function as well as healing: walking comfort, bladder and bowel control, pressure symptoms and the activities you want to resume. If exercises remain confusing or symptoms persist, ask whether pelvic health physiotherapy would help. A clear plan should tell you both what to do next and what information would make the team reconsider it.

Common questions

Does removing the uterus remove the need for pelvic floor care?

No. The pelvic floor still has roles in bladder and bowel control and support. Discuss symptoms and an appropriate rehabilitation plan with your team.

Can I use my usual vaginal trainer straight after surgery?

Do not resume an internal device without specific postoperative advice. The timing depends on healing and the procedures you had; gentle external contractions do not establish that insertion is safe.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
