How to keep a bladder diary: a practical guide for women

Turn a few ordinary days into a clear record for your appointment, without guessing missing entries or changing your routine.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

A bladder diary records drinks, bathroom visits, urine amounts, urgency and leaks across the day and night. Clinicians often request at least three days. Use the chart and instructions your care team provides, keep your usual routine, mark missing measurements honestly and bring the completed record to your appointment.

What to take away

  • Include nights and ordinary activities, not only convenient daytime hours.
  • A missed measurement should be marked as missing, never as zero.
  • A diary supports assessment; its numbers do not diagnose you.

What a diary can—and cannot—tell you

A bladder diary helps quantify patterns such as urgency, leakage, daytime frequency and nighttime trips. It can support assessment and later show whether treatment has changed those patterns. The European Association of Urology recommends a record lasting at least three days. Read its bladder diary guidance.

The aim is to show what happens in your life. One small urine measurement does not prove that your bladder is small, and one busy bathroom day does not establish a diagnosis. The record belongs alongside your symptoms, medical history and clinical assessment.

Get the practical pieces ready

BAUS suggests using a measuring jug and checking the capacity of cups or mugs you use regularly. Choose ordinary days and record drinks as well as urine output. Its input/output chart explains the method.

Before starting, ask which dates and chart your clinician wants. Have a paper sheet or accessible note ready, plus a container kept specifically for urine measurement. Use milliliters if the chart requests them. Label your usual mug’s capacity in your notes so “one mug” does not change meaning halfway through.

Plan for work and travel. If you cannot measure away from home, keep recording the visit and mark the volume as unmeasured. A realistic record with clear gaps is more helpful than an invented complete one.

What to record each time

Kingston and Richmond NHS recommends recording the time, type and amount of each drink, and the time and amount of urine passed during both day and night. It also records urgency and leakage. See its diary instructions.

  • Drink: time, type and measured or estimated amount.
  • Bathroom visit: time and urine volume, if measured.
  • Urgency: your chart’s score, or a brief description if no scale is provided.
  • Leak: time, approximate size and what was happening.
  • Sleep: bedtime, waking time and any nighttime visits.

Use the supplied scoring definitions consistently. Do not convert “strong urgency” into a guessed number of minutes you could have waited. If a symptom needs more explanation, add a short note rather than squeezing it into the wrong column.

Notice the difference between a dash, an estimate and an unmeasured visit. Keeping those distinctions makes the information easier to interpret. You can add your own context without turning every entry into a paragraph.

Handle missing information honestly

Manchester University NHS’s diary instructions say to mark a bathroom visit even when you cannot measure the urine amount. Read its practical recording advice. “Not measured” is not the same as passing no urine.

If you forget a drink or an overnight trip, add what you remember and label uncertainty. Do not backfill an exact volume from memory. Note unusual circumstances, such as travel or being unwell. Ask whether your clinician wants another day rather than silently replacing the awkward day with an easier one.

Keep the first record representative of your usual habits unless your clinician explicitly asks you to test a change. If you alter drinks or training, record when the change began.

Bring questions as well as numbers

Choose two or three questions for your appointment: “What pattern do you see?” “Do I need any tests?” and “Which change should we try first?” Bring your medicines list and a description of the symptom’s impact. Our guides to urgency and nighttime urination can help you describe those concerns.

This educational guide does not interpret your diary. Do not wait to finish it if you need urgent care. Inability to pass urine needs immediate assessment, while blood in urine needs prompt medical help. NIDDK retention guidance and NHS blood-in-urine advice explain these signs.

Common questions

Do I need to measure every visit perfectly?

Try to follow your clinician’s instructions, but label missed measurements honestly. Record that a visit happened rather than entering a guessed value or zero.

Can I use a phone instead of paper?

Yes, if it captures the required fields and you can share or print the record for your appointment. Follow any format requested by your care team and consider where private health notes are stored.
