Yoga, Pilates and your pelvic floor: where do Kegels fit?

Choose movement you enjoy while keeping your specific pelvic floor treatment goals clear.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Yoga and Pilates can be part of an enjoyable movement routine, but a general class does not assess your pelvic floor or replace an individual treatment plan. A trial of pelvic yoga found no clear superiority over general conditioning for reducing total urine leakage. Choose suitable instruction, discuss symptoms and keep prescribed pelvic floor practice separate.

What to take away

  • A class name does not tell you whether it fits your symptoms.
  • Yoga research does not establish that every yoga or Pilates class treats leakage.
  • Ask for modifications and avoid pushing through pain or vaginal heaviness.

Separate fitness goals from treatment goals

You might choose a class for movement, social contact, flexibility or simply because you enjoy it. Those are valid reasons. Treating urine leakage or pelvic pain is a more specific goal, and it deserves a clear plan of its own.

Before joining, write down what you hope the class will help you do. “Return to an activity I enjoy” is different from “stop leaking during a jump.” Bring the second goal to a pelvic health professional as well as discussing it with the instructor. That keeps expectations realistic without making every class feel like a medical appointment.

What does research say about yoga for leakage?

A 2024 randomized trial compared a structured pelvic yoga programme with general stretching and strengthening in women aged 45 and older with urinary incontinence. Both groups improved, and the main analysis did not show clear superiority of yoga for total leakage frequency.

The programme involved trained instruction and specific adaptations. It was not a test of every yoga style, an ordinary online class or Pilates. Participants with several conditions, including symptomatic prolapse and recent pregnancy, were excluded. The findings therefore cannot establish which class is suitable for every reader.

When a headline says “yoga fixes leakage,” look for who took part, what the comparison group did and what outcome actually changed.

What about Pilates?

Pilates classes differ in exercises, equipment, pace and teaching. A mat class, a reformer session and individual rehabilitation can be very different experiences. Ask what is included rather than assuming that the word “core” guarantees a pelvic floor treatment.

Imperial College Healthcare describes pelvic health exercise programmes that may combine specific pelvic floor work with wider strengthening or stretching, chosen according to the person's needs. That is a useful distinction: general movement and targeted rehabilitation can sit together, while serving different purposes.

If your physiotherapist has prescribed exercises, bring the names or a copy of the plan to your instructor. Ask which class movements overlap and which are additional. Avoid quietly replacing the prescribed work because two exercises have similar descriptions.

Questions to ask before your first class

  • What experience do you have adapting sessions for pelvic symptoms?
  • Can I use a simpler version or rest without disrupting the class?
  • Are there long holds, jumps or movements that need advance modification?
  • If a movement causes symptoms, how will we adapt the session?
  • Would an individual session help us agree modifications first?

You can keep the conversation brief: “I am managing a pelvic health issue and may need to change some exercises.” Share more detail privately if you wish. You do not need to explain intimate symptoms to the group.

If you are pregnant, recently postpartum or recovering from surgery, use advice specific to that situation. A class advertised as gentle still needs to fit your current restrictions.

Should I squeeze throughout the class?

Do not interpret every mention of the pelvic floor as an instruction to maintain a constant clench. St George's guidance on muscle overactivity explains that excessive pelvic floor exercise without enough relaxation can contribute to problems. Muscles need the ability to release as well as contract.

If an instruction conflicts with your treatment plan, ask for clarification. “Should I maintain this contraction, release between repetitions or leave it out?” is a practical question. If a movement brings on pain, leakage or a feeling of pressure, stop that movement and arrange a review if symptoms persist.

Make the next session informative

After class, record one or two relevant observations: which movement was comfortable, which needed adapting and whether symptoms occurred. You do not need a detailed score for every pose. Bring recurring patterns to your clinician.

For example, “The standing sequence felt fine; pressure began during the long hold” gives more useful information than “Pilates is bad for me.” Together, you can decide what to modify and what to keep. Read pelvic floor and lifting for a similar approach to gym activities.

Common questions

Can yoga replace my prescribed Kegels?

Do not swap treatments solely because a class mentions the pelvic floor. Discuss whether your particular class and clinical goals justify changing your programme.

Is every gentle class suitable for pelvic pain?

No class label can establish that. Discuss pain and any restrictions with your clinician, and ask the instructor how individual movements can be adapted.
