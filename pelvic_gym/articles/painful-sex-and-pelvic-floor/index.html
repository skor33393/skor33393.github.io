Painful sex and the pelvic floor: what to discuss and what may help

Pain during sex deserves an explanation and a care plan. Stronger contractions are not automatically the answer.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Pelvic floor muscles can contribute to painful sex, particularly when they tighten involuntarily or have difficulty relaxing. Pain can also come from tissue changes, infection, skin conditions or other pelvic problems. Frequent or severe pain deserves assessment. Avoid forcing painful penetration or starting a strengthening routine to solve an unknown cause.

What to take away

  • Pain is a reason to change the approach, not a requirement for progress.
  • Muscles may be involved, but a matching symptom does not establish the cause.
  • Your examination and treatment should respect your choices and comfort.

The pelvic floor is one possible part of the explanation

Pain with sex may be felt at the entrance, deeper inside the pelvis, or afterward. ACOG describes several possible causes, including hormonal tissue changes, skin conditions, infection, childbirth-related problems and conditions such as endometriosis. More than one factor can matter.

Before choosing an exercise, describe the pattern. Is the discomfort associated with touch at the entrance, deeper penetration, a particular position or the time afterward? Does dryness seem relevant? Do tampons or examinations also hurt? These observations can help a clinician decide what to assess; they cannot identify the cause by themselves.

You do not have to prove that the pain is severe enough to deserve help. If it changes what you are comfortable doing, it belongs in the conversation.

When muscle tightening is involved

With vaginismus, muscles tighten involuntarily around attempted vaginal insertion. It can happen with penetration, a tampon or an examination, and it is not something a person chooses. The NHS explains this automatic response and describes specialist treatment that may combine education, relaxation and other approaches.

A description that sounds familiar is not a diagnosis. Pain at the vulva can also occur without an obvious visible change; the NHS vulvodynia guide explains why persistent or recurring vulval pain needs assessment. You should not have to see a lesion or swelling before asking for care.

Our tight-versus-weak guide explains why squeezing harder is not an appropriate default response to pain. The useful question is what treatment fits the findings.

Protect comfort while arranging care

You can pause penetration or choose activities that do not hurt. ACOG's sexual health guidance discusses allowing time for arousal, using appropriate lubricant and considering activities without penetration. If using condoms, check lubricant compatibility; water-based or silicone options are generally appropriate, while oils can damage latex.

Agree on an easy way to stop or change activity before it starts. For example: “If I say pause, I need us to stop and check in.” You do not owe anyone a further attempt to see whether the pain will disappear. A supportive conversation can focus on what feels comfortable now, without making penetration the measure of success.

Lubricant may reduce friction, but persistent pain still needs investigation. Do not repeatedly treat presumed thrush, insert weights or force a stretch without understanding the problem. Bring the names of any products you have tried to the appointment.

Make the assessment easier to use

ACOG advises seeing a clinician for frequent or severe pain during sex. Assessment may include your medical and sexual history, an examination and tests when appropriate. See its guide to painful sex.

You can prepare a brief note rather than explaining everything from memory:

  • Where the pain is and what it feels like.
  • When it began and whether it is changing.
  • Any bleeding, discharge, urinary symptoms or skin changes.
  • Relevant childbirth, surgery, medications or menopause symptoms.
  • What you want to be able to do comfortably.

Before an examination, discuss what is proposed and what would help you feel in control. Ask about a chaperone or support person and explain that you want each step described. Our pelvic health appointment guide outlines the choices involved.

What might treatment involve?

Treatment depends on the cause. The NHS describes care involving different specialists for vulval pain, with options that may include medication, physiotherapy and psychological or psychosexual therapies. This does not mean the pain is imaginary; it means care may need to address several aspects of the experience.

Vaginal trainers or dilators may be used in some specialist plans, but they are not the same as strengthening weights. The NHS vaginismus guidance places their initial use under trained supervision. Ask what the tool is for, how to use it comfortably and when to stop.

Review progress using your own priorities, such as less discomfort with daily touch or greater comfort during an examination. There is no requirement to achieve a particular sexual activity by a deadline. If the plan increases pain or feels unmanageable, return for an adjustment.

Common questions

Should I do more Kegels if sex hurts?

Not without understanding the cause. Stronger contractions are not a general treatment for painful sex. Seek assessment and ask whether your muscles need help with relaxation, coordination or another issue.

Do vaginal trainers mean I have to push through discomfort?

No. Ask a qualified clinician for an individualized plan and clear stopping rules. A trainer is not a test of how much pain you can tolerate.
