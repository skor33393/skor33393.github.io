Postpartum pelvic floor recovery: a practical guide after birth

Build a recovery plan around your birth, symptoms and daily life, with space for rest, questions and specialist help.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20267 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Postpartum pelvic floor recovery is individual. Gentle movement and appropriate pelvic floor exercises may be part of recovery, but pain, leakage, bowel-control problems or a vaginal bulge deserve attention. Follow your maternity team’s advice, especially after a complicated birth, surgery or a significant tear, and progress according to symptoms.

What to take away

  • Your recovery needs a personal plan, not a deadline from someone else’s experience.
  • Report bladder, bowel, heaviness and pain symptoms clearly.
  • A postnatal appointment is an opportunity for support, not an automatic exercise clearance.

Start with your own birth and symptoms

After birth, the pelvic floor can feel weak or difficult to activate. Sensation and control may change, and some women need pelvic health physiotherapy. RCOG describes these early changes. A friend’s quick return to running tells you very little about what your body needs.

Write down what you notice in ordinary life: a leak when standing up with your baby, pressure after a walk, discomfort on the toilet, or pain when sitting. Record the activity and what happened afterward. Specific examples are easier to discuss than “I do not feel normal,” and they can help you describe the support you need.

What a gentle start can look like

After an uncomplicated birth, the NHS says gentle activity can begin when you feel ready. A complicated delivery or caesarean needs more individualized advice before strenuous exercise. Discuss higher-impact exercise with your healthcare professional. Read the NHS guidance on activity after birth.

Make the first decision small: what comfortable activity fits today? That might be a short walk or a brief movement break. Keep your maternity team’s instructions close by rather than trying to combine several online recovery schedules. If activity brings on leakage, heaviness or pain, record it and ask for guidance before increasing the challenge.

Practical support counts. Ask someone to carry shopping, hold the baby during an appointment or make it possible for you to rest. Recovery planning can include those arrangements alongside exercise.

Leaking after you stand up from the toilet?

Describe this timing separately from a leak with coughing or a sudden urge on the way to the bathroom. A few drops after getting dressed and a larger unexplained leak may need different questions. The timing alone does not prove weakness or identify where urine remained.

NIDDK lists leakage among possible symptoms of incomplete bladder emptying, but leakage alone does not establish retention. If emptying is a concern, ask whether a postvoid residual measurement is appropriate; this is a clinical measurement of urine remaining after you pee.

Take this short description to your midwife, GP, physiotherapist or continence service:

  • When: while still sitting, on standing, or after walking away.
  • Alongside it: urgency, slow flow, straining, a feeling of incomplete emptying, burning or pain.
  • Change: whether the problem started or worsened after an exercise or another change to your plan.

A useful message is: “I leak after leaving the toilet. This happens separately from urgency. Can we review emptying and the exercise instructions before I add more repetitions?” If a prescribed maneuver repeatedly worsens symptoms, pause that maneuver and ask its prescriber for advice. Do not increase squeezing or force urine out to test a theory.

Seek immediate care if you cannot pass urine or have severe abdominal pain. For a fuller explanation of assessment, see difficulty emptying and urinary hesitancy. An app cannot check residual urine; strengthening should fit the assessed problem.

How pelvic floor exercises fit into recovery

RCOG advises pelvic floor rehabilitation after birth and notes that specialist physiotherapists can assess bladder, bowel and pelvic symptoms. Its pelvic floor information explains the role of physiotherapy. Ask your midwife or physiotherapist when to start in your circumstances and what an appropriate contraction should feel like.

Do not use pain as a sign that an exercise is working. If squeezing hurts, feels confusing or is difficult to release, ask for help rather than adding repetitions. Our technique guide can support a plan you have been given; our relaxation guide explains the other part of muscle control.

Still having urgent leaks months after birth?

Persistent leaks deserve a review even if you have practiced every exercise you were given. Describe whether leaking follows a cough or jump, comes with a sudden urgent need, or happens in both situations. The NHS explains that treatment depends on the type of incontinence; bladder training may be offered for urge incontinence, while muscle training has a different role.

Do not interpret “keep practicing” as a requirement to continue an unclear or ineffective plan indefinitely. Ask for a review of what has changed, which symptoms remain and what the next step should be. A useful message is: “I have followed this plan, but urgent leaks still interrupt my day. Can we review the type of leakage, my technique and the bladder-management part of treatment?”

Bladder training is a structured treatment, not simply forcing yourself to wait until an accident happens. The NHS describes a course usually lasting at least six weeks; that is not a guarantee of recovery or a reason to postpone review of worsening symptoms. If squeezing hurts or you have trouble emptying, discuss that before increasing contractions or delaying toilet visits.

Use the bladder training versus Kegels comparison to prepare questions, and the urgency guide for assessment and warning signs. Pelvic Gym can help organize a strengthening routine when that is part of your agreed care plan; an app cannot diagnose the leak type or replace the review.

Include bladder, bowel and wound care

After birth, constipation can make toilet visits uncomfortable. The NHS recommends fluids, fiber-rich foods and avoiding straining; ask your clinician if constipation persists. Tell them about stool leakage or difficulty controlling your bowel, too. The NHS covers bladder and bowel changes after birth.

If you had a third- or fourth-degree tear, follow the care and follow-up plan you were given. Increasing wound pain, unusual odor, discharge, redness or fever can indicate infection and should be checked promptly. Bowel-control problems also warrant a conversation about specialist help. RCOG explains care after a significant tear.

Make your postnatal appointment useful

You can seek help before your scheduled postnatal check. The NHS encourages asking about physical problems whenever they bother you; the routine check is another opportunity to discuss recovery. See the NHS post-pregnancy body guide.

Bring a short list:

  • What happens with walking, lifting, coughing and toilet visits?
  • Do I need a pelvic health physiotherapy referral?
  • What should make me reduce activity or call for help?
  • How should I decide when to progress, and when will we review?

If you leave without understanding the plan, ask for it in plain language. It is reasonable to say, “I need to know what to do this week and who to contact if symptoms continue.”

Know when recovery symptoms need urgent attention

Seek emergency medical care now for severe or worsening pelvic pain, fainting, or sudden or very heavy vaginal bleeding. Pelvic pain with fever needs urgent medical advice. Do not put these symptoms down to exercise or ordinary recovery. The NHS pelvic pain guidance describes urgent warning signs.

This educational guide does not assess your healing. A manageable next step is to share one specific symptom with your maternity team rather than waiting for a universal “back to normal” date.

Common questions

Do I need pelvic floor advice after a caesarean?

You may still have questions or symptoms after pregnancy and a caesarean. Ask your maternity team about your own recovery and exercise plan; the delivery method alone is not a substitute for assessment.

Does the postnatal check mean I can return to any exercise?

No single appointment date tells you that every activity will feel comfortable. Discuss symptoms, healing and the activity you want to return to, then ask how to progress and when to review.

Should I just do more Kegels if urgent leaks continue months after birth?

Ask for a review rather than automatically increasing exercises. Urgency, stress leakage and mixed leakage can need different management. Bring your symptom record and current plan, especially if there is pain or difficulty emptying.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
