Pelvic floor exercises for women: a practical starting guide

Understand the muscles, choose a sensible first step, and build a routine that includes both lifting and letting go.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Pelvic floor exercises train the muscles that help support the pelvic organs and control bladder and bowel openings. A useful starting point is learning a comfortable squeeze, lift and full release. Your symptoms and ability to relax matter: pain or difficulty emptying your bladder deserve assessment before more strengthening.

What to take away

  • Learn the release as carefully as the squeeze.
  • Choose a plan around your symptoms and ability, not an online challenge.
  • A professional can check your technique and help you decide what to practice.

What are pelvic floor exercises?

Your pelvic floor is a group of muscles across the base of the pelvis. It helps support the bladder, bowel and uterus and contributes to continence. Kegel exercises are one way of training these muscles: you contract them, then let them relax. The NIDDK guide to Kegels explains their role in bladder and bowel control.

The movement is small. There is no need for a dramatic body position or a visible workout. Before choosing a routine, make your goal specific: perhaps you want to understand a clinician's instructions, feel more confident about technique, or seek help for leaking during a cough. Those are different starting points, and it helps to name yours.

When can strengthening help?

Pelvic floor muscle training is a recommended treatment for stress urinary incontinence, which means leaking with pressure such as coughing or exercise, and for mixed incontinence. NICE recommends a supervised programme lasting at least three months for these conditions. Supervision includes checking both contraction and relaxation and adapting practice to the individual. See NICE's pelvic floor guidance.

This does not mean every pelvic symptom is a request for stronger squeezes. University Hospitals Birmingham describes several causes of pelvic pain, including an overactive pelvic floor. If pain is your main concern, start with assessment rather than a strengthening target. Our guide to a tight versus weak pelvic floor explains why the distinction needs care.

Learn the movement before counting repetitions

Choose a comfortable supported position. Imagine preventing wind from escaping while gently drawing the muscles around the vagina inward and upward. Keep breathing, avoid pushing down, and let the muscles release completely afterward. Health New Zealand describes this as a squeeze and lift and advises seeking help if you cannot feel that action. Read its pelvic floor exercise guide.

Treat your first attempt as a chance to notice the movement, not a strength test. Can you tell when you are contracting? Can you tell when you have stopped? What is difficult to describe? Write down one uncertainty to bring to an appointment. A sentence such as “I can squeeze, but I cannot feel the release” is more useful than deciding you are simply bad at the exercise.

Practice away from the toilet. Do not make stopping urine flow part of your exercise routine; it can interfere with bladder emptying. For a fuller walkthrough, read how to do Kegel exercises.

Turn advice into a workable plan

Ask for instructions you can actually follow at home. A plan should tell you the position, number of contractions, hold time, rest time and sessions per day. If you have been given a programme, use those details rather than adding another routine from a video or app.

  • Write down the starting point. Keep your current instructions in one place.
  • Choose a reminder. Pick a quiet part of the day when you can pay attention.
  • Track comfort. Record questions and symptoms, not only completed sessions.
  • Know the review date. Decide when you will discuss progress with your clinician.

For example, your reminder might say “follow my prescribed practice after breakfast.” That is clearer than “do more Kegels.” A checklist or guided timer can support an agreed routine, but it cannot confirm that your muscles are moving correctly.

When to get individual help

Seek help if you cannot locate the muscles, have persistent leakage, feel pain during exercise or sex, or have difficulty emptying your bladder or bowel. These are reasons for professional assessment, as outlined by Queensland Health. Stop an exercise that hurts instead of trying to complete the count.

You do not need a perfect explanation before booking. Bring your symptom notes and any exercises you have tried. Ask what the first goal should be and how you will know whether the plan needs changing. Progress can start with getting a clear answer to that question.

Advice also needs to fit your stage of life: explore pelvic floor concerns around menopause or the questions to ask after hysterectomy when relevant.

Common questions

Do I need equipment to start?

Usually, basic pelvic floor practice does not require equipment. If you cannot identify the movement, ask for a technique assessment before buying a device.

Are Kegels the only pelvic floor treatment?

No. Treatment depends on the problem and can include relaxation, bladder or bowel strategies, physiotherapy and other options. A strengthening routine should match your assessment.
