Bowel urgency after childbirth: what if I cannot wait?

Being dry from the bladder does not settle a bowel-control concern. Describe the urgency and ask for a plan that fits daily life.

By Pelvic GymNovember 7, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

If bowel urgency after childbirth leaves you little time to reach a toilet, keeps recurring or threatens your return to work, contact your GP, maternity team or pelvic health clinician. Explain whether you have urgency alone, actual leakage or both. Do not assume time or extra Kegels will solve it without reviewing the cause and your current plan.

What to take away

  • Describe bowel and bladder symptoms separately.
  • Request review when urgency or leakage limits ordinary activities.
  • An app cannot assess birth injury, bowel function or the right treatment.

Describe urgency separately from leakage

Urgency means the need feels difficult to postpone. Leakage means stool actually passes when you did not intend it to. You can need help with urgency before an accident occurs. Also mention staining, loss of wind control or trouble emptying if those happen, without assuming they share one cause.

NIDDK distinguishes urge leakage from leakage without awareness and describes several possible contributors, including bowel disorders and muscle or nerve injury. Childbirth can be relevant, but an online account cannot identify what happened in your case.

Tell the clinician if bladder control seems fine. That is useful information, not a reason to dismiss the bowel problem.

Ask for a review that addresses the bowel problem

NHS guidance recommends seeing a GP for bowel-control problems or persistent changes in stool. If you already attend pelvic health physiotherapy, report that the bowel symptoms are not improving and ask whether further medical or specialist assessment is needed.

Bring your birth and recovery information if available, including any repair or later treatment, but do not try to infer a tear grade from symptoms. The RCOG information about diagnosed third- and fourth-degree tears concerns a specific injury; having urgency does not establish that diagnosis.

A useful opening is: “Since giving birth, I sometimes have very little warning before a bowel movement. It is limiting where I can go. Can we review the cause, my treatment and what to do before work resumes?”

Bring four useful details

DetailWhat to tell the clinician
Warning and controlWhether you usually reach the toilet, have near misses or experience leakage
Stool and emptyingLoose or hard stool, straining, pain, blood or a sense of incomplete emptying
TimingWhen it began, changes since birth and any connection you notice with meals or medicines
Daily impactTravel, caring tasks, shifts or time away from a toilet

This is an appointment prompt, not a diagnostic score or a prescribed bowel-training diary. Use a brief factual note; you do not need to monitor every sensation all day. Include the exercises or medicines already recommended and what happened when you followed the plan.

Make a practical plan before returning to work

Tell your treating team what your shift actually involves: travel time, toilet access and whether you can leave a task quickly. Ask which symptoms need review before returning and what interim arrangements could help. Where appropriate, discuss toilet access and breaks with a manager or occupational-health service. Local workplace processes vary.

Plan for the journey and first shift as well as the work itself. Knowing the toilet locations, allowing time to stop and having spare supplies if you choose can reduce logistical uncertainty. These steps do not treat the cause or replace assessment.

Do not respond by avoiding all food or drastically restricting drinks. Ask for individualized diet or fluid advice if you think they affect your symptoms. Keep any existing medical fluid restriction in place until your clinician advises otherwise.

Why more repetitions are not automatically the answer

NIDDK describes treatment choices that depend on the cause, including dietary or medication changes, bowel training, suitable pelvic floor exercise and biofeedback. A routine used for urine leakage may not fully address your bowel symptoms. Ask what each part of your plan is meant to improve and how progress will be reviewed.

If exercise produces pain, pressure or difficulty releasing, tell your clinician rather than increasing the dose. Pelvic Gym provides guided strengthening workouts when that approach is appropriate. It does not measure anal muscle function, assess a birth injury or replace clinical biofeedback. Confirm any app routine with the professional managing your recovery.

For related preparation, see postpartum pelvic floor recovery and difficulty controlling wind.

When to seek prompt care

NHS bowel guidance advises urgent assessment for black or dark-red stool or bloody diarrhea. Severe or worsening pelvic pain, fainting or heavy vaginal bleeding needs emergency care. Pelvic pain with fever or difficulty passing urine or stool needs urgent medical advice; see NHS pelvic-pain warning signs. A routine follow-up date should not delay that care.

Common questions

Can I have bowel urgency without urine leakage?

Yes, report bowel and bladder function separately. Being dry from the bladder does not establish the cause or severity of a bowel-control problem.

Should I wait until my postnatal symptoms improve by themselves?

Seek review if urgency or leakage persists, affects daily life or is not improving with your current plan. A general recovery timeline cannot predict your individual outcome.

Will Pelvic Gym treat postpartum bowel urgency?

The app cannot diagnose or treat the cause of bowel urgency. It may support a suitable strengthening component only when your clinician confirms that it fits your plan.
