7 common Kegel exercise mistakes—and what to do instead

A practical troubleshooting guide for technique, rest, dosage and expectations, without turning discomfort into a challenge.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

Common Kegel mistakes include bearing down, gripping nearby muscles, holding your breath, skipping the release and practicing during urination. Another is assuming every pelvic symptom needs stronger squeezes. Use a comfortable, assessed technique and an individual dose; if pain, emptying problems or uncertainty persist, get professional help.

What to take away

  • A repetition includes a full release, not just a squeeze.
  • More contractions are not automatically a better plan.
  • Pain and uncertainty deserve assessment rather than persistence.

1. Pushing downward instead of lifting inward

A Kegel should involve an inward lift, not a downward strain. If the instruction “squeeze” makes you brace and push, pause. Try describing the direction of the movement rather than trying harder. Health New Zealand specifically advises against bearing down and recommends professional help when a definite squeeze and lift cannot be felt.

Next step: bring one clear question to an assessment: “Am I lifting or pushing?” A longer workout will not answer that question.

2. Turning it into a thigh or buttock workout

Clamping the legs together or squeezing the buttocks can make the effort feel substantial without clarifying the pelvic-floor movement. Mayo Clinic advises focusing on the pelvic floor and avoiding unnecessary tightening of the abdomen, thighs or buttocks. See its Kegel how-to guide.

Next step: use a supported position and notice what moves on the outside. Treat that observation as information to discuss, not proof that you have isolated the muscles correctly. Our step-by-step technique guide gives you a short checklist.

3. Holding your breath to finish the count

A longer hold is not useful if you have to stop breathing to complete it. Mayo Clinic recommends breathing freely during Kegels. If the count takes all your attention, you may miss the moment when comfortable technique ends.

Next step: write down when breath-holding begins and ask whether the hold should be adjusted. Do not treat a number displayed by a timer as an instruction to override discomfort. You can pause the session and preserve that question for your clinician.

4. Skipping the release between squeezes

The movement has a finish as well as a start. The Better Health Channel emphasizes a full release and rest between contractions.

Next step: give the rest interval its own place in your written plan. If you can feel the squeeze but cannot identify the release, say so at your assessment. Avoid carrying a constant background clench through the rest of your day.

5. Practicing by stopping urine flow

“Imagine stopping urine” is a cue, not a reason to repeatedly interrupt peeing. Leeds Teaching Hospitals advises against midstream exercise because it can interfere with normal bladder function.

Next step: choose a practice location away from the toilet. If you have trouble starting or finishing urination, bring that up with a healthcare professional rather than using the toilet as a strength test. Emptying symptoms need their own assessment.

6. Adding repetitions whenever progress feels slow

Combining several routines makes it harder to describe what you actually practiced. Keep a list of the programmes you use and discuss it before adding another. Use your individual plan to decide what belongs in your day.

Next step: keep one written prescription. List every exercise source you currently follow and ask which ones belong together. If you miss a session, avoid improvising a marathon catch-up. Use our frequency guide to organize reminders around the dose you have been given.

7. Treating every pelvic symptom as weakness

Painful or difficult-to-relax muscles may need a different approach. Royal United Hospitals Bath explains that an already-tense pelvic floor can be difficult to contract and can be associated with bladder, bowel and sexual pain.

Next step: stop exercises that hurt and seek assessment. Describe the location, timing and triggers of symptoms rather than labeling yourself “weak” or “tight.” Read tight versus weak pelvic floor to prepare questions, not to diagnose yourself.

Use this list without overchecking

You do not need to monitor seven things during every repetition. Pick the main uncertainty, write it down and ask for help resolving it. Keep the routine simple enough to remember. The aim is comfortable, appropriate practice that supports your goals, not a flawless performance or an unbroken streak. You can bring this list to an appointment and circle the item that best describes your uncertainty.

Common questions

Should Kegels feel sore afterward?

Do not use soreness as a target. If exercise causes pain or new symptoms, stop and ask a qualified clinician to review your technique and whether strengthening is appropriate.

Can I fix every mistake by following a video?

A clear demonstration can help explain the movement, but it cannot assess your internal contraction or relaxation. Persistent uncertainty is a good reason for individual guidance.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
