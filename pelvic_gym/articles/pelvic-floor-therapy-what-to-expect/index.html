Pelvic floor therapy: what to expect at your first appointment

Know what the conversation, examination and treatment plan can involve—and the choices you have throughout.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

A first pelvic floor therapy appointment usually begins with your symptoms, health history and goals. An examination may be offered, including an internal assessment when relevant, but it requires your consent. Treatment can include education and individualized exercises. You can ask for explanations, discuss alternatives and request a chaperone.

What to take away

  • Bring examples of the activities your symptoms affect.
  • An internal examination is a choice to discuss, not a test you must pass.
  • Leave knowing what to practice, what to stop and how follow-up works.

Before you book or attend

Pelvic health physiotherapists work with bladder, bowel, sexual and pregnancy-related problems. You do not need to know which muscle is responsible before seeking help. University Hospitals Sussex explains the scope of pelvic health care.

When booking, check whether the appointment is by phone, video or in person, how long to allow, and whether you should complete any forms. A remote first appointment can gather your history and explain possible examinations; an in-person visit may follow if needed, as described by DynamicHealth's NHS service. Policies vary, so use your own clinic's instructions.

If you need an interpreter, accessible room, support person or particular communication approach, ask in advance. You do not need to wait until an uncomfortable moment to explain what would help.

If the clinic sends a questionnaire that feels difficult to complete, note the questions you would rather discuss in person. Bringing an incomplete form is preferable to inventing an answer.

The first conversation: what matters to you?

Expect questions about symptoms, previous treatment and relevant medical history. Rather than searching for the correct medical term, bring examples: “I leak when I lift my toddler,” “I feel pressure after standing,” or “penetration hurts at the entrance.” These tell a clearer story than “my pelvic floor is bad.”

Prepare a short page with:

  • When the concern began and whether it has changed.
  • Activities that bring it on or ease it.
  • Relevant surgery, pregnancies, births and medications.
  • Exercises or products you have already tried.
  • The activity you most want to make easier.

Birmingham Women's and Children's NHS recommends bringing relevant letters, medication details and any requested diary or questionnaire. If you have been asked to complete a bladder diary, bring the actual record; you do not need to summarize it perfectly.

What might an examination involve?

The assessment depends on your concern. It may look at movement and the painful area; a vaginal or rectal examination may be proposed to assess pelvic floor function. Imperial College Healthcare describes this individualized process.

Before agreeing, ask what the examination will assess and how the result would change treatment. You can say, “Please explain each step before you do it,” or “I would like to discuss the examination today and decide later.” Tell the clinician if a particular position, touch or past experience makes you apprehensive; you can choose how much detail to share.

Sussex's appointment information states that an internal examination is optional and that a chaperone can be provided. Ask your clinic about these arrangements. You can withdraw consent and ask to stop.

Therapy is more than a list of Kegels

Your plan might include movement practice, education, lifestyle advice or other techniques selected for your findings. Imperial's patient guide describes several treatment approaches rather than one standard routine. Read why tightness and weakness are different questions if you are expecting strengthening automatically.

Ask your therapist to connect each instruction to your goal. “This is intended to help with the leak during lifting” is easier to use than collecting exercises without knowing their purpose. If the plan is too complicated to fit around work or caring responsibilities, say so before leaving.

Leave with a plan you can repeat accurately

For each exercise, record the position, movement, repetitions, rest and frequency. Ask what discomfort should make you stop and who to contact with questions. Demonstrate or explain the instructions back in your own words so that any misunderstanding can be corrected while you are together.

Finally, confirm the follow-up arrangement: whether you must book it, what to track and when the approach will be reviewed. Keep a note of one useful change in daily life as well as what you practiced. If nothing changes, that information belongs at the review too. Therapy is a process of assessment and adjustment; your job is to give an honest account, not to produce a perfect progress report.

Common questions

Do I have to have an internal examination?

No. Ask why it is being offered, discuss alternatives and decide whether to consent. Declining may limit what can be assessed, but you can still discuss symptoms and appropriate care.

Should I cancel if I am on my period?

Some pelvic health services can still see you during your period. Check your clinic’s policy and your own comfort rather than assuming cancellation is necessary.
