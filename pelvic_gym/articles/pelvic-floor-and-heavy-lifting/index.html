Pelvic floor and heavy lifting: what to do about symptoms

A practical approach to leaking or pressure during weights, shopping and other everyday lifting.

By Pelvic GymOctober 24, 20264 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

There is no single lifting limit that fits every woman's pelvic floor. Symptoms, training history and recovery from pregnancy or surgery matter. If lifting brings on leakage, pain or vaginal pressure, modify the task and seek assessment. Research in experienced young lifters cannot establish safety for everyone or predict long-term effects.

What to take away

  • Describe the lift and symptoms instead of labeling every weight unsafe.
  • Load, repetitions, rest and breathing are useful topics for a review.
  • Follow individual restrictions after birth or surgery, and seek help for new symptoms.

Does heavy lifting always damage the pelvic floor?

A 2024 crossover study measured pelvic floor function after a lifting session and after rest in 47 experienced lifters aged 18–35 who had not given birth. It found no significant difference in the measured immediate changes. This is useful evidence against assuming every heavy session causes an immediate loss of measured muscle function.

However, it did not establish long-term safety, study every lifting intensity or represent women after childbirth, with prolapse, or recovering from surgery. It also found that stronger squats and deadlifts did not necessarily mean stronger pelvic floor muscles. Your gym numbers cannot substitute for assessment.

Make the symptom specific

“Lifting bothers my pelvic floor” is a starting point. Add enough detail to help a professional understand what happened:

  • What were you lifting, and from what height?
  • Did the symptom begin on the first repetition or later?
  • Was it leakage, pain, vaginal heaviness or something else?
  • Did it settle after stopping, or continue afterward?
  • Was the same task previously comfortable?

A note such as “leaked on the final repetitions of the second set” is more informative than a general statement that exercise is impossible. You do not need to repeat a provoking lift to prove the pattern.

What can you discuss changing?

The Pelvic Floor First resistance exercise guide describes adjusting weight, position, repetitions and rest when pelvic floor problems are present. It also discusses breathing out with effort. These are options to individualize with a suitable professional, not a universal list of forbidden exercises.

Bring your actual programme to the discussion. Ask which change to try first and what would count as an acceptable response. If you reduce the weight, alter the movement and double the repetitions all at once, it becomes harder to understand which change mattered.

For an ordinary shopping task, a practical change might be splitting the load or using a trolley. At the gym, it might mean temporarily choosing a different exercise while the provoking movement is assessed. The aim is a workable plan for your real activities.

Where does breathing fit?

Wirral Community Health teaches breathing out during the effort of a movement, such as rising from a squat or lifting. Ask your clinician or qualified trainer to help you coordinate this at a manageable load.

Do not use one breathing cue as permission to override postoperative restrictions or persist through symptoms. Equally, a cue that works for a light household task is not a complete plan for maximal competitive lifting. Explain the activity you actually want to return to, including its demands.

If someone has advised a pelvic floor contraction before lifting, ask when to start and when to release. Clear timing is more useful than trying to hold a continuous squeeze through an entire workout.

Include work and home in the plan

Gym sessions are only part of the picture. Caring for a child, moving stock at work or carrying laundry may matter more to your day. Make a short list of the three lifting tasks you cannot easily avoid and discuss those first.

For each task, identify what can realistically change: load size, carrying distance, lifting height, equipment or help from another person. If you have a temporary lifting restriction, ask for examples relevant to those tasks and a review date. “Avoid heavy lifting” needs translating into instructions you can use at home.

When to get assessed

Persistent leakage, pain or a vaginal bulge or pressure should be discussed with a healthcare professional. The lifting study's authors specifically recommend specialist assessment for pelvic floor symptoms in exercisers. You do not need to wait until you stop all activity.

Bring both your symptom notes and your training goals. Ask what you can continue, what to modify and how progression will be reviewed. Our guides to exercise with prolapse and pelvic health physiotherapy can help prepare that conversation.

Common questions

Is there a universally safe weight in kilograms?

No single number accounts for your symptoms, medical history, lifting task and training experience. Ask for advice that describes the activity as well as the weight.

Should I stop all strength training if I leak?

Arrange assessment and discuss changes to the activity that triggers symptoms. A symptom does not by itself tell you which exercises you must stop permanently.
