Pelvic heaviness or vaginal pressure: what to do next

Describe the feeling, notice accompanying symptoms and get a clear follow-up plan without trying to diagnose yourself.

By Pelvic GymOctober 26, 20263 min read

Educational content · Our editorial standards · Sources linked below

The short answer

New or persistent pelvic heaviness deserves assessment. Prolapse is one possible cause, but a feeling of pressure alone cannot identify it or tell you whether your pelvic floor needs strengthening. Arrange medical advice and mention changes in bladder or bowel function, pain, bleeding or a vaginal bulge.

What to take away

  • A sensation of heaviness is a symptom, not a prolapse stage or muscle-strength score.
  • A normal initial check does not replace follow-up when symptoms persist or change.
  • Sudden inability to urinate or severe pelvic pain needs prompt medical care.

Why the same feeling can have different explanations

NHS prolapse guidance includes vaginal heaviness, pressure and a bulge among possible symptoms. It recommends seeing a GP for a lump or other prolapse symptoms. The presence or absence of a lump you can see does not settle the cause by itself.

The NHS pelvic pain guide also describes pressure or a heavy ache and notes that bowel, bladder, infection and gynecological problems can have overlapping symptoms. You do not need to choose a diagnosis before making an appointment.

When should you seek care?

Arrange a medical appointment for persistent or recurring pressure, especially if it is changing what you can do. Explain whether there is pain, a bulge or a change in emptying your bladder or bowel.

Get urgent medical advice for pelvic pain with fever, unusual bleeding or discharge, difficulty peeing or pooing, or possible pregnancy. Seek emergency care for severe or worsening pelvic pain, fainting or heavy vaginal bleeding. These distinctions follow NHS pelvic pain guidance; use your local urgent or emergency service.

NIDDK advises immediate medical care if you cannot urinate. Do not wait to try an exercise session or a home test first.

Make a short symptom record

Write a few useful observations without repeatedly checking inside the vagina or trying to push through the sensation:

  • When it began and whether it is constant or comes and goes.
  • Where you feel it and your own words for it: heaviness, dragging, fullness or pressure.
  • Whether standing, lifting, bowel movements or another activity changes it.
  • Any pain, bleeding, bladder or bowel changes, and whether pregnancy is possible.
  • What it is stopping you doing and what you have already tried.

Bring your previous test results or appointment summary if available. A clear timeline is often easier to discuss than a long list of possible diagnoses found online.

What if your first appointment did not find a cause?

Ask what was checked and what the result does and does not rule out. If the sensation persists, contact the service again with the change or lack of improvement. You can say: “The pressure is still happening after standing, and it is affecting my day. What is the next step?”

Discuss whether a pelvic health, gynecology or other assessment is appropriate to your symptoms. Ask when to return if the plan does not help, and which new symptoms should prompt earlier contact. You do not need to prove a diagnosis to request a review.

During any proposed examination, ask what it is intended to assess and discuss consent, comfort and a chaperone. Our appointment guide explains how to prepare.

Why not start with harder Kegels?

Pressure does not tell you how well your muscles contract and release. Choosing an intensive routine before understanding the symptom may send you down the wrong path. Avoid treating a change in the sensation after one squeeze as a diagnosis.

Start with assessment and the plan that follows it. If strengthening is recommended, Pelvic Gym offers guided workouts to help organize practice. The app cannot identify the cause of pressure or replace an examination. See our guide to tension and weakness for the distinction.

Common questions

Can I have pressure without seeing a bulge?

Yes, pressure does not require a visible bulge, but that observation cannot establish or exclude prolapse. Describe persistent symptoms to a clinician.

Should I keep checking to see if anything has changed?

Repeated self-examinations are not a reliable monitoring plan. Ask your clinician what to observe and when a reassessment is needed.

Your next step

Start with the right support.

If you have pain, pressure, or trouble relaxing, a pelvic health professional can help you work out what your muscles need. An exercise app cannot make that assessment.
